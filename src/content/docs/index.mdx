In a magnetron discharge the plasma above the target is not uniform. It breaks up into
**spokes**: ionization zones a few millimetres above the racetrack that rotate along it at
kilometres per second. Their speed, direction, number and shape change with power, pressure,
magnetic field and target material. They carry part of the electron transport across the
magnetic field, and they help the sputtered metal ions reach the substrate.

These notes follow a research programme that studies the properties of spokes by computation.
It starts from the experimental picture in the review by Hecimovic and von Keudell (2018) and from
the gaps that review names. A second aim is to reproduce the published magnetron simulations,
one model family at a time, against the numbers their papers report.

## At a glance

<CardGrid>
	<Card title="The experiments" icon="magnifier">
		Spokes rotate against the E×B drift at low power and with it in HiPIMS, about ten times
		slower than the drift. At very high power the plasma turns homogeneous again.
		No probe gets inside a spoke, so its density is still not known.
	</Card>
	<Card title="The simulations" icon="laptop">
		2D particle-in-cell codes reproduce spokes at DC densities. At HiPIMS density, 3D PIC is out
		of reach. Fluid theory is linear and has neither gas depletion nor secondary electrons, and
		global models average the spokes out.
	</Card>
	<Card title="The approach" icon="list-format">
		A ladder of models, each checked against the one below and against measurements: a 0D
		global model, a 2D drift-fluid, 2D PIC-MCC, and a coupled fluid-PIC model.
	</Card>
	<Card title="Honesty about sources" icon="information">

		reference list). See [About these notes](/about/).
	</Card>
